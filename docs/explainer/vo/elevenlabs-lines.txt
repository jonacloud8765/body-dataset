Most days are ordinary.

Nothing happens. Nothing needs to.

Then, sometimes, something doesn't fit.

When a threat appears, a person has to respond.

Time stops. One moment, one threat.

Broadly, there are five ways a response can go.

Fight: move toward the threat and confront it. It can work, when trained and appropriate.

Flight: move away and increase the distance, when escape is possible.

Freeze: the body stops, while the world keeps moving.

Sometimes stillness hides you, or buys time.

Held too long, the opening closes. It can follow sensory overload.

Submit: lower, yield, hand control to the threat.

Posture: look bigger, sound firm, show resistance.

In people and animals alike, it can stop violence before it starts.

The appearance of strength is as important, in many cases, as strength itself.

A threat reacts to what it perceives.

Animals do it too.

Signals like this are how we read each other.

One metaphor describes people by how they relate to violence.

Sheep: ordinary people who live peacefully and avoid violence.

Many are uncomfortable even thinking about it, and that's normal.

Wolves: people who use violence against others.

For gain, for power, or out of cruelty. Criminals, terrorists, predators.

Someone on the bench stands up. Nothing about them looks different.

Sheepdogs: protectors, willing to confront violence to defend others.

Soldiers, police officers, and others willing to step in.

Wolf and sheepdog can both use force.

What differs is intent: one preys, the other protects.

And control: the sheepdog is expected to use force responsibly.

Most days, the sheepdog's life looks like everyone else's.

The difference is readiness, not aggression.

It's a metaphor, not a scientific classification of people.

Calm, but ready is a state, and states change.

Back to the start. Now, from the inside.

Condition White: relaxed, unaware, attention somewhere else.

Here is what you missed the first time.

When something happens, it takes longer to notice and to react.

The problem isn't fear. It's attention.

Condition Yellow: relaxed alert. Head up, phone away.

Nothing is wrong. You simply notice what's around you.

Calm, but ready.

When something happens, you see it sooner and respond sooner.

Condition Orange begins when a specific possible threat appears.

Attention narrows onto it. Everything else becomes less important.

Adrenaline rises. The heart speeds up.

And precise tasks start to get harder.

Condition Red: the threat is immediate. The action phase.

Big movements stay strong. Precise ones fall apart.

Complex thinking can decline too.

Someone shouts, “This way!” The voice, and the exit, may never register.

Options narrow to fight or flight. Effects vary from person to person.

Condition Black: the system overloads.

Signals overlap. Nothing forms a clear picture.

Action becomes erratic, or stops altogether.

Freeze: the same response from the start.

Extreme arousal doesn't make you more ready. It can take decisions away.

Arousal shapes what you notice, what your body can do, and how you decide.

These ranges are approximate, and they differ from person to person.

Put it together: threat, awareness, arousal, condition, response, decision.

Notice late, and there's less time to respond, and fewer options.

Notice early, and there's time: time to stay in control, and to choose.

Preparedness is not aggression. It keeps the decision in your hands.

Recognize early.

Know your options.

Keep control.

Calm, but ready.
